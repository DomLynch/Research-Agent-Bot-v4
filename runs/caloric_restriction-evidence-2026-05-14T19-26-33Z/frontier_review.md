## The lens

Caloric restriction's outcomes are not uniform but are critically sex-specific and context-dependent, as seen in macroadenoma attenuation in male mice (1.71 ± 0.26; P < 0.01) but not females (2.35 ± 0.25; P = 0.71) in Cancer Prevention Research 2013, and paradoxical immune responses with TNF-α/IL-6 increases in Oxidative Medicine and Cellular Longevity 2015 versus decreases in Cancer Research 2012, challenging simplistic benefit narratives and demanding personalized, mechanistic studies.

## Already known — do not publish

- Caloric restriction reduces body weight and improves insulin sensitivity in mice (fact 7)
- Caloric restriction increases cortisol and decreases testosterone in humans (fact 20)
- Caloric restriction reduces hs-CRP and IL-6 in some human studies (facts 2, 15)

## Tensions / contradictions

- Sex-specific efficacy in cancer attenuation: males show macroadenoma reduction with CR (fact 8, Journal of Bone and Mineral Research 2010), while females do not (fact 1, same journal) but respond to VF- diet
- Inconsistent inflammatory markers: CR increases TNF-α and IL-6 in Oxidative Medicine and Cellular Longevity 2015 but decreases IL-6 in Cancer Research 2012, possibly due to differing doses (e.g., 40% CR vs. other levels) or durations
- Bone health trade-offs: CR improves metabolic markers (fact 7) but elevates bone resorption and marrow adiposity in Journal of Bone and Mineral Research 2010 (facts 10, 13)

## Evidence gaps

- Female reproductive aging under CR: only one study (fact 3, PNAS 2011) addresses oocyte aneuploidy at 40% CR, with no data on dose-response or interactions with menopause
- Short-term vs. long-term immune modulation: evidence from Gut Microbes 2017 shows microbiome shifts at 8-36 weeks, but inflammatory outcomes vary by timing (e.g., 2-week CR in Immunometabolism 2019)
- Specific subgroups like aged mice with insulin mutations (fact 22, Endocrinology 2016) showing paradoxical insulin increases, which are underexplored

## Reviewer objections to anticipate

- Heterogeneity in CR protocols (e.g., 40% CR vs. other percentages) and model organisms (e.g., C57BL/6 mice vs. humans) limits comparability across studies
- Lack of longitudinal human data on bone health trade-offs (e.g., from Journal of Bone and Mineral Research 2010) may reduce translational relevance
- Small sample sizes in key studies like Cancer Prevention Research 2013 could bias meta-analytic results

## Suggested next extractions

- Harvest data on CR in female models post-menopause or with genetic predispositions (e.g., from Endocrinology 2016)
- Explore CR doses below 40% (e.g., 20-30%) and durations under 4 weeks for immune outcomes, as in Nutrients 2020
- Investigate CR interactions with microbiome changes in Gut Microbes 2017 for inflammatory pathways
