## Already known — do not publish

- Gait speed decreases with age, with prevalence of gait disorders rising from 10% in ages 60-69 to over 60% in ages over 80.
- Low gait speed is associated with worsening activities of daily living (ADL) and higher mortality risk (e.g., HR 1.20 per 0.1-m/s decrease).
- Gait speed is a predictor of hospitalizations, emergency visits, and functional decline in older adults.

## Tensions / contradictions

- Resistance training shows large effect sizes for improving gait speed (d > 0.99), while whole-body vibration fails to improve gait scores (SMD = 0.04), indicating intervention-specific efficacy that lacks comparative data.
- Measurement accuracy of gait speed varies significantly between laboratory (ICC 0.79-0.91) and real-world settings (ICC 0.57-0.88), posing challenges for consistent assessment and generalizability.
- Associations between gait speed and outcomes hold in patients with good performance status (ECOG 0 or 1), but evidence in poorer status groups or specific comorbidities is limited.

## Evidence gaps

- Dose-response relationships for interventions like exercise (e.g., resistance training intensity) on gait speed and longevity are underexplored, with hints only showing broad improvements.
- The impact of dietary patterns on gait speed reduction is minimally studied; only one hint suggests an association, needing more randomized trials.
- Subgroups such as cancer patients, individuals with sarcopenia (calf circumference >31 cm), or those with dementia lack detailed gait speed-longevity data, as current hints focus on general aging populations.
