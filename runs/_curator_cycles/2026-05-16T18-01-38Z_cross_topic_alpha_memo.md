## Cross-topic thesis

The efficacy of metformin and exercise in cancer prevention is highly context-dependent, mediated through pathway-specific interactions (particularly involving mTOR and inflammatory axes like TNF), which vary with metabolic state, subgroup characteristics, and combinatorial interventions, challenging uniform clinical application.

## Why this is surprising

Despite robust preclinical signals (e.g., metformin reducing tumor burden by 72% in mice, exercise modulating inflammatory pathways), clinical evidence often shows null or inconsistent effects (e.g., no association with colorectal cancer risk in diabetic cohorts), revealing that unappreciated modulators such as metabolic context, dosing, and interactions between interventions critically determine outcomes.

## Strongest counter-thesis

The synthesis relies on heterogeneous evidence from limited preclinical and clinical studies with varying designs, populations, and endpoints (e.g., cross-species comparisons, single trials), which may undermine causal inferences and generalizability to human biology.

## What this changes

This highlights the necessity for precision approaches in research and clinical practice, emphasizing stratified trials based on metabolic phenotypes, subgroup analyses, and investigation of combination therapies (e.g., metformin+exercise) to define therapeutic niches and avoid one-size-fits-all assumptions.

## What would falsify it

Large-scale, well-controlled clinical trials demonstrating consistent benefits of metformin or exercise across all contexts, subgroups, and endpoints without evidence of pathway-specific or context-dependent modulation, coupled with null effects in preclinical models.
