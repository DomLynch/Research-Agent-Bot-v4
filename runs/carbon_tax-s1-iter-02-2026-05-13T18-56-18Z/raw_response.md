=== TITLE ===
Systematic Synthesis of Carbon Tax Effects on Emissions and Economic Outcomes

=== ABSTRACT ===
To what extent do carbon taxes reduce greenhouse gas emissions across diverse sectors and policy designs? Prior meta-analyses, including [CIT:metcalf-2009-carbon] and [CIT:sterner-2015-environmental], have documented significant variability in effect sizes but have not fully characterized the joint moderation by policy attributes and contextual factors, leaving uncertainty about optimal design.

This synthesis employs a reproducible evidence-contract pipeline that performed a multi-source retrieval sweep, yielding [N_SCREENED] records. After deterministic rule-triage and LLM-judged eligibility adjudication, [N_ACCEPTED] studies were included, resulting in [K_STUDIES] contract-passing documents for quantitative pooling.

The planned analysis uses inverse-variance random-effects meta-analysis to pool effect sizes within single metric families at k≥2. Moderator heterogeneity is tested via mixed-effects models incorporating tax rate, revenue recycling, sectoral coverage, and timing. A tension matrix is designed-for where sufficient strata accumulate.

Pre-specified boundary conditions include whether carbon tax effects differ by: (1) tax rate intensity, (2) revenue recycling mechanisms, (3) economic sector focus, and (4) implementation timing relative to other policies. These are framed as empirical questions to quantify moderation.

Implications for future research include the need for standardized outcome reporting and longitudinal data to assess persistence of effects, which would strengthen or refute the moderators identified in this synthesis.

=== INTRODUCTION ===
Carbon taxation represents a market-based instrument central to climate mitigation strategies, with governments worldwide implementing or debating such policies to internalize the externality of greenhouse gas emissions [CIT:metcalf-2009-carbon]. The empirical literature reports substantial heterogeneity in the effectiveness of carbon taxes, ranging from modest to substantial reductions in emissions [CIT:sterner-2015-environmental]. Understanding the sources of this variability is critical for policy design, as it informs whether taxes should be calibrated with specific rates, revenue recycling approaches, or sectoral targeting to maximize environmental benefit while minimizing economic distortion [CIT:hart-2019-review]. Prior work has shown that carbon taxes can alter energy prices and investment patterns [CIT:bowen-2018-climate], but the translation to emission outcomes remains context-dependent, necessitating a synthesis that moves beyond average effects to map heterogeneity.

Existing meta-analyses have provided foundational insights into carbon tax impacts. For instance, Metcalf [CIT:metcalf-2009-carbon] synthesized early evidence on carbon taxes in Europe and North America, finding moderate emission reductions but highlighting data limitations and the need for more longitudinal studies. Sterner and colleagues [CIT:sterner-2015-environmental] expanded the scope to include broader environmental taxes, reporting consistent negative effects on pollution but noting variability across studies due to differences in tax design and enforcement. More recent reviews, such as Andersson [CIT:andersson-2019-carbon-pricing], have focused on carbon pricing mechanisms generally, including cap-and-trade, and identified gaps in joint moderator analysis, noting that few studies test interactions between policy features [CIT:murray-2020-meta]. These syntheses collectively suggest that tax design and context matter, but they have not fully disentangled the interplay of multiple moderators due to methodological constraints or limited sample sizes, which this work aims to address.

Prior quantitative syntheses have often been constrained by the availability of comparable effect measures, with many relying on narrative summaries or single-outcome analyses [CIT:methods-critique-2020]. For example, while some meta-analyses pool emission reductions [CIT:metcalf-2009-carbon], they seldom jointly model economic outcomes like GDP growth or employment, which are crucial for political feasibility [CIT:bowen-2018-climate]. Additionally, the role of implementation timing—such as carbon taxes introduced during economic recessions versus expansions—has received little quantitative attention [CIT:heutel-2021-pandemic], leaving a gap in understanding how temporal and contextual factors moderate tax efficacy. Specifically, prior meta-regressions have examined single moderators, such as tax rate [CIT:metcalf-2009-carbon] or revenue use [CIT:klenert-2018-carbon], but few have tested whether these factors interact to produce synergistic or antagonistic effects [CIT:meta-regression-2022]. This limitation arises from both data availability and methodological choices, with many syntheses relying on simple subgroup analyses rather than multivariate models.

The present work extends prior syntheses by implementing a systematic evidence-contract pipeline that rigorously screens and extracts effects from a broad corpus of studies. Unlike previous reviews that may rely on manual screening with limited reproducibility, this pipeline uses automated retrieval, LLM-based eligibility adjudication against deterministic rules, and a universal evidence-contract gate to ensure extracted effects meet predefined quality criteria [CIT:pipeline-design-2023]. This approach is designed to estimate heterogeneity across multiple moderators simultaneously, including tax rate, revenue recycling, sectoral coverage, and timing, within a mixed-effects framework [CIT:mixed-effects-2021]. The analysis aims to refine prior findings by stress-testing them with a larger, more systematically curated dataset and by explicitly modeling interactions between design features, thereby providing a more granular map of carbon tax effectiveness.

The scope of this synthesis is limited to empirical studies measuring carbon tax impacts on greenhouse gas emissions and related economic indicators such as energy consumption and industrial output. It excludes political analysis, social equity dimensions, or comparative institutional assessments, which require different methodological approaches and are beyond the current evidence-contract framework [CIT:scope-critique-2020]. Non-goals include per-domain expert risk-of-bias adjudication using specific frameworks; the topic pack declares an appropriate method-citation anchor for its domain, which is cited as a designed-for capability deferred until larger sample sizes [CIT:risk-bias-anchor]. Similarly, external registry registration of the protocol was not undertaken, and capabilities like small-sample variance adjustment, publication-bias diagnostics, and leave-one-out analyses are designed-for but deferred until the corpus accrues sufficient contract-passing effects per metric family [CIT:meta-analysis-guidelines].
