PMC Copyright notice PMCID: PMC12990209 NIHMSID: NIHMS2149120 PMID: 34509882 The publisher's version of this article is available at Epilepsy Behav Abstract Individuals who experience recurrent spontaneous seizures often show behavioral and physiological comorbidities. Those with epilepsy are at a high risk for bone fractures (independent of seizure-related falls) and show a higher rate of a diagnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder. The neural subset-specific (NS) Pten knockout (KO) mouse has an epilepsy phenotype, has been characterized to show autistic-like deficits, and has an osteoporosis phenotype. The current study examined the effect of a vitamin D enriched diet (20,000 IU VD) in the NS- Pten KO and wildtype mouse. Mice were placed onto a vitamin D enriched diet at 4 weeks of age and maintained on that diet throughout testing. Behavioral testing began at 6 weeks of age and included tests for general activity, anxiety, repetitive behaviors, social behaviors, and memory. Results indicated that a vitamin D diet attenuated hypoactivity levels in male KO mice ( p &lt; 0.05). In a social partition task, vitamin D increased sociability in male wildtype mice, ( p &lt; 0.05). Most significantly, vitamin D fortified diet increased percent survival in KO animals and decreased the level of microglia marker IBA-1 and mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) downstream targets pS6 and pAKT. A high vitamin D diet did not reverse bone deficits in male or female KO mice. Overall, these findings suggest that a vitamin D enriched diet had a significant impact on the behavioral phenotype of NS- Pten KO mice, suggesting that dietary manipulations could be a potential therapeutic option for autistic-like behavior. Introduction Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder in which approximately 50 million people experience recurrent and unpredictable seizures [ 1 ]. Anticonvulsant treatments can help manage seizures; however, over half of all individuals report negative side effects and approximately a third of individuals with epilepsy are resistant to all pharmaceutical therapies [ 2 ]. Common antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) like carbamazepine have been shown to decrease vitamin D levels [ 3 – 6 ]. Thus, further investigation of treatments with fewer side effects is needed. Individuals with epilepsy also suffer from several comorbidities, with one study finding a cooccurrence with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) from 4.7–47.4% depending on the type of epilepsy [ 7 ]. In addition, people with epilepsy have a higher rate of bone fractures than the general population [ 8 , 9 ]. These fractures are independent of falls related to seizures. A reduction of bone mineral density is often associated with a lack of vitamin D [ 10 ]. People with epilepsy often are deficient in vitamin D levels [ 11 ]. Despite the clinical studies that individuals with epilepsy have a reduction in bone mineral density and have low levels of vitamin D, only a few studies have examined these measurements in pre-clinical models of epilepsy. Mutations in PTEN are associated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities like macrocephaly, intellectual disability, and epilepsy [ 12 , 13 ]. In addition, mutations in PTEN are also implicated in mediating behavioral comorbidities associated with epilepsy like autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [ 12 , 14 ]. The phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) gene is a negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, and when deleted results in hyperactivation of the signaling pathway [ 15 ]. Hyperactivation of mTOR signaling has been implicated in epilepsy caused by genetic mutations in components of the pathway, including Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) and PTEN, as well as in acquired models of epilepsy [ 15 , 16 ]. The current study utilizes a neuron-specific Pten (NS- Pten KO) mouse model, in which the gene is deleted in a subset of neurons in the hippocampus, cortex, and cerebellum. Previous work in our lab has shown deletion of PTEN in this mouse model results in an epileptic phenotype in addition to the development of autistic-like behaviors in adulthood, including alterations in sociability and repetitive behavior [ 17 , 18 ]. The NS- Pten KO mouse also has a significant reduction in bone mineral density in the proximal tibial metaphysis and midshaft compared to WT mice [ 19 ]. Therefore, this model will allow us to determine treatments that could reverse epilepsy comorbidities such as autistic-like behaviors and bone phenotypes. In the present study, we investigated whether a vitamin D enriched diet could be a potential dietary intervention to attenuate the behavioral deficits associated with comorbidities of epilepsy and the deletion of PTEN, including tests for general activity, anxiety, repetitive behaviors, social behaviors, and memory. We also examined whether vitamin D enriched diet could reverse the bone growth abnormalities in the NS- Pten KO mice. We examined weight and the rate of mortality in the wildtype and knockout mice after vitamin D supplementation. We also examined several bone measurements for the mice. Previous findings found that a high intake of vitamin D (20,000 IU) helped to rescued hypocalcemia in CYP27B1 KO mice [ 20 ]. This study focuses on the potential of a dietary therapy where treatment mice received a vitamin D diet for a total of 5 weeks. 2.0. Materials and methods 2.1. Animals Female heterozygous mice (+/−) were bred with male heterozygous mice (+/−) to produce male and female wild type (WT +/+) and knockout (KO −/−) mice. Behavioral testing began at 6 weeks of age, with tests being conducted in order of least invasive to most invasive as follows: open field, elevated plus maze, nose poke, social partition, delayed fear conditioning. All animals were bred and housed at Baylor University. The colony room was maintained at a controlled temperature of 22° C in a 12-hour light and 12-hour dark diurnal cycle. All animals had access to food and water ad libitum. Testing took place during the light phase and between the hours of 8am and 5pm. All procedures were performed in accordance with Baylor University Institutional Care and Use Committee and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. Beginning sample size for animals were as followed: NTotal = 123 (Male, CTL, WT = 16; Male, CTL, NS- Pten KO = 16; Male, vitamin D, WT = 13; Male, vitamin D, NS- Pten KO = 14, Female, CTL, WT = 17; Female, CTL, NS- Pten KO = 19; Female, vitamin D, WT = 15; Female, vitamin D, NS- Pten KO = 13) 2.2. Dietary Manipulation Beginning at 4 weeks of age, mice in the vitamin D treatment condition were placed on a vitamin D rich mouse chow (Provided by Envigo: 2020, Teklad Global Soy Protein-Free Rodent Diet, pre-extruded, (999.714 g/Kg); Vitamin D3, cholecalciferol (500,000 IU/g), (0.037 g/Kg); Red Food Color, (0.25 g/Kg) equivalent to 20,000 IU/kg [ 20 ] and remained on this diet for a total of 5 weeks ( Figure 1 ). Mice were given approximately 200 g of food each week and weekly intake of the amount consumed was calculated each week. All other mice remained on the same diet without the vitamin D supplement chow and contained approximately 1500 IU/kg of vitamin D3 (Provided by Envigo: 2020, Teklad Global Soy Protein-Free Rodent Diet). In addition, mice were weighed weekly. Figure 1. Open in a new tab Feeding and behavioral testing timeline. 2.3. Open Field The open field task examined changes in the animal’s activity levels and general anxiety. After mice were weighed, markings were made on their tail to identify them for testing. The mice were then allowed to acclimate to the room for 30 minutes before testing. The open field apparatus was made up of a clear acrylic arena (40 × 40 × 30 cm). The lighting in the room was maintained at 100 lux, and the background noise was kept consistent at 60 dB. Mice were placed into the testing cage for 30 min. Analysis of activity levels was by a computer-operated optical animal activity system (Fusion by Omnitech, USA). After testing, mice were placed into a clean holding cage with bedding. Once all the mice from the same cage were tested, mice were returned to their home cage from the clean cage. The arena was cleaned using a 30% isopropyl alcohol solution in between testing sessions. 2.4. Elevated Plus Maze Baseline anxiety levels were assessed on the elevated plus maze. The elevated plus maze apparatus consisted of four arms (30 × 5 cm), with two opposite arms containing acrylic walls and the other two arms open, with all arms 40 cm above the floor. Light levels (30 lux) and background noise (60 dB) was kept constant during testing. Subjects were tested for a 10 min with the experimenter not present. Ethovision XT video tracking software (Noldus, Netherlands) was used to track the frequency of entries and duration in the center and each of the four arms of the apparatus. Simultaneous video scoring was recorded using Pinnacle video capture software (Corel, Canada). An experimenter blind to treatment offline scored head-dips in the open arms and rearing activity. After testing, mice were placed into a separate cage with tested animals until all mice from that cage had been tested, then all mice were returned to their home cage. The testing apparatus was cleaned between tests with a 30% isopropyl solution. 2.5. Nose Poke As a test of repetitive behavior, the nose poke test was conducted in a clear acrylic arena (40 × 40 × 30) with a clear plastic board inserted into the bottom. The board had 16 equidistant holes of 1-inch diameter and 3/4 inch deep. Mice were placed individually into the apparatus for 10 minutes. Testing was scored by a trained observer blind to experimental condition. A nose poke was counted when the mouse placed their nose into the holes as far as their eyes. Between each mouse, the apparatus was cleaned using 30% isopropyl alcohol. Mice that were tested were placed in a clean holding cage until all mice from the home cage were tested. Following the last mouse from that home cage being tested, all mice were placed back into their home cage. 2.6. Social Partition As a test of social behavior, the social partition task was used to measure the duration and frequency of interactions with a familiar or novel mouse. As the methods outlined in Spencer et al. (2011), animals were kept in a cage which was divided using an acrylic insert with 0.6 cm diameter holes. On the other side of the divider, a sex-, age-, weight-matched conspecific was placed for 24 hours. Testing occurred the following day and consisted of three 5 min tests. The first and third test conditions were with the familiar mouse in which the test mouse was housed with overnight. The second test condition was with a novel mouse. A trained experimenter live scored the test using the software program Ethom to score the duration and frequency of sniffing events. 2.7. Delayed Fear Conditioning To assess learning and fear-related memories, delayed fear conditioning was conducted. An operant conditioning chamber (26 × 22 × 18 cm) with 2 sides made from acrylic, and 2 sides made of metal as well as a metal grid floor that delivered a mild shock was used. This chamber was also placed inside another enclosed chamber to isolate the sound (Coulbourn Habitest ® from Coulbourn Instruments). All trials were measured using a FreezeFrame 3 automated system from Actimetrix (RRID: SCR_014429). The trials and stimulus for this test are as previously described by our lab [ 21 ]. 2.8. Western Blot Preparation After the final behavioral test, were sacrificed and the hippocampi were rapidly dissected and stored at −80°C until tissue processing. Tissue preparation for the hippocampi was done for total homogenized hippocampus [ 18 ]. The following concentrations were used for the primary antibody from Cell Signaling: GFAP (1:1000), IBA-1 (1:500), pS6 (1:500), S6 (1:500), pAKT(1:500), AKT(1:500), actin(1:1000). Secondary concentrations are as followed: IBA-1(1:500), all others used 1:20,000. After tissue processing, homogenized samples were stored at 4°C for the remainder of the project. Western Blot procedure was run as previously described [ 22 ]. 2.9. microCT Analysis For microCT analysis, mice were euthanized by rapid decapitation and then stored at 20°C until later analyzed. Mice were scanned at the proximal and mid diaphysis with a vivaCT40 microCT scanner (Scanco Medical, Switzerland) at 10.5 μm voxel resolution using the following settings: (55 kVp, 145 μA, 2048 samples, 1000 proj/180°, 200ms integration time). Analysis of trabecular parameters in the tibial metaphysis (BV: bone volume, TV: total volume, BV/TV: bone volume/ total volume, Tb.N: trabecular number, and Tb.Th: trabecular thickness) and cortical parameters in the tibial midshaft (Ct.Th: cortical thickness, endocortical volume, and TMD: tissue mineral density) were performed as previously described [ 23 ]. 2.10. Statistical Analysis All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25) and the graphs were created using GraphPad (Prism 7). The open field, elevated plus maze, nose poke, social partition, day 2a of delayed fear conditioning tasks, and all bone phenotype analyses were analyzed using a 3-way ANOVA (Genotype [WT, NS- Pten KO] x Diet [CTL, vitamin D] x Sex [male, female]). All significant two-way interaction results were followed up by creating interested fixed factor variable groups (i.e. Treatment and Genotype), analyzed using a one-way ANOVA with the interested dependent variable, and any statistically significant findings followed up with post hoc Tukey’s HSD tests. For three-way interactions, the data was split by sex and Tukey’s post hoc analysis was run with individual group identifiers (i.e. Male CTL WTs). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to analyze the results for day 1 of fear conditioning (with “time” as a within subject’s variable that contained the two levels of freezing behavior, baseline and tone), and for day 2b, an ANOVA was used to further investigate the interaction between genotype and time by using the baseline (min 1–3) and tone (min 4–6) as dependent variables. To analyze potential weight differences, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was utilized (with “weight” as a within subject’s variable that contained 5 levels of weight, one for each week of dietary treatment). In addition, for this analysis, the data was split by sex. A Kaplan Meier survival chi-square analysis was used to analyze survival rates during the 5 weeks of treatment and testing. Western Blots were analyzed by normalizing the antibody to their actin level and for phosphorylated proteins also the level of the total antibody (i.e. pS6 was also normalized to total S6) and are expressed as a percent over average CTL. Only animals tested in at least one behavioral test were included in analysis. The level of significance was set at p &lt; 0.05 for all comparisons. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Animal exclusions and deaths for each test are reported in the results section of each behavioral test. Discussion This study expanded and replicated many of the previous findings of the epileptic comorbidities in the NS- Pten knockout model such as behavioral deficits. In the elevated plus maze, male NS- Pten KO animals displayed an increase in activity levels and no differences in anxiety levels [ 18 , 24 , 25 ]. We also found that NS- Pten KO mice exhibited an overall decrease in repetitive behavior in a nose poke task, which were in in line with a previous finding from our lab [ 18 ]. There was a genotype x diet effect in the nose poke task, however the only differences found were between WT and the NS- Pten KO groups, not within. This suggests that this may be driven more by the genotypic differences, rather than a vitamin D supplement effect. Pten KOs had reduced sociability in the presence of both a familiar and unfamiliar mouse across all trials of the social partition task, as previously found [ 18 , 24 , 26 ]. There was also a sex difference effect, where we found that female mice had a decrease in sociability compared to male mice. We found that vitamin D attenuated some, but not all phenotypic changes exhibited by the NS- Pten KO animals. In the elevated plus maze, hyperactivity exhibited by NS- Pten KO mice was attenuated by the vitamin D diet. In addition, vitamin D led to a decrease in anxiety in male NS- Pten KO mice. In the open field test, NS- Pten KO mice displayed hypoactivity and a decrease in number of rearings, which were rescued by exposure to the vitamin D. Previous findings have found an increase in activity levels and rearings in the NS- Pten KO mouse [ 18 , 24 ]. The difference in activity levels could be due to the timing of when the task was performed, as Kwon and colleagues measured activity levels at 4 weeks and 8 weeks of age, while we examined activity levels at 6 weeks of age [ 24 ]. This dose used in our experiments was chosen because high concentrations of vitamin D (20,000 IU) have been shown to rescue calcium level deficits in mouse model of hypocalcemia [ 20 ] in addition to this dose increasing the bone volume and strength in C57BL/6J mice [ 27 ]. Rowling et al. 2007, utilized mice lacking 25-hydroxycholecaciferol-a-hydroxylase (CYP27B1) that have impaired weight gain, hypocalcemia and poor bone mineralization (2007). Mice received either a 1,000, 10,000, or 20,000 IU diet of vitamin D3. They found that the 10,000 or 20,000 IU vitamin D diet had a significant increase in the 25(OH)D3 and calcium serum levels in addition to having greater weight and mineralization compared to the 1,000 IU vitamin D. Williamson and colleagues used 8 week old C57BL/6J mice and fed them either a standard diet (1000 IU/kg of vitamin D3) or a diet containing 8,000, or 20,000 IU/kg of vitamin D3 and remained on this diet for a total of 4 weeks [ 27 ]. After analyzing the tibia of the mice for its morphology, three-point binding, and reference point indentation, it was found that the highest vitamin D diet (20,000 IU/kg) had a greater failure displacement and had significantly less stress at the point of failure [ 27 ]. Micro computed tomography analysis found that 20,000 IU/kg of vitamin D had greater bone volume and lower trabecular separation compared to the other groups [ 27 ]. Vitamin D treatment has shown therapeutic potential in several disease models including cancer, kidney disease, mood disorders, and epilepsy [ 28 – 31 ]. Individuals with epilepsy had a 30–40 % reduction in seizures when given a vitamin D supplementation compared to controls [ 11 , 32 ]. Studies have also found that some epileptic populations have an increase in the number of seizures in the months of the year where there is less sunlight [ 33 , 34 ]. In murine models, vitamin D treatment efficacy has shown therapeutic potential in several disease models including cancer, kidney disease, mood disorders, and epilepsy [ 28 – 31 ]. Treatment of vitamin D resulted in a higher seizure threshold susceptibility, and when seizures were induced animals displayed fewer severe seizures [ 35 – 37 ]. Furthermore, mice that lack the vitamin D receptor (VDR) were more likely to have seizures, had more severe seizures, and a decrease in survivability following seizure induction [ 38 ]. We replicated many of our NS- Pten bone phenotypes we found in our prior study [ 19 ]. We found that KOs had a reduction in percent BV/TV, bone volume, and trabecular number in the proximal metaphysis bone region. We also replicated our previous results where we reported an increase in cortical expansion in the proximal metaphysis and midshaft region in the NS- Pten KOs. Our results point to bone region-specific differences where the metaphysis was characterized by decreased trabecular bone mass, yet there was endocortical expansion in both locations. One difference in this study compared to our previous study is that we included females. We found that many of the differences were greater in females than in males. Other mouse models of Down’s Syndrome and Marfan’s syndrome have recently demonstrated sex-specific bone phenotypes for each syndrome and point to the need to include female mice to understand the relationship between sex in mediating clinical disorders [ 39 , 40 ]. We did not detect any impact of the high vitamin D diet on bone growth. This was surprising since two previous studies found that a high diet in vitamin D impacted bone growth [ 39 , 40 ]. It is important to mention that the 20K diet the animals were fed did not fully rescue the bone phenotype in the Rowling et al., 2007 study of hypocalcemia. They revealed complete recovery of cortical bone in their knockout mice fed either the rescue or 10K diets but only partial recovery of trabecular bone measures (e.g. 40% lower bone volume, 20% lower trabecular thickness, and 23% increase in trabecular separation). One difference between the current study and the prior study, is that the mice in the prior study were on the diet for 12 weeks compared to 5 weeks in our study. Future studies could include a longer period of fortified vitamin D diet. Most significantly, we found that vitamin D impacted the survival rate in the NS- Pten KO mouse as only CTL NS- Pten KO animals died before the end of the study. The mice have a progressive epilepsy phenotype and show an increase in mTORC1 and mTORC2 signaling [ 15 , 41 , 42 ]. Inhibition of mTOR through rapamycin decreases the decrease in the number of spontaneous seizures and prolongs the life of the mice [ 16 , 17 ]. One study found that even late administration of rapamycin significantly increases the number of mice who live to postnatal week 16 to 86% compared to 33% of the NS- Pten KO mice that received the sham treatment [ 42 ]. The mice with late administration of rapamycin also had a decrease in the astrocyte marker GFAP and the microglia protein marker IBA-1. The rapamycin studies clearly demonstrated that inhibition of mTOR can increase longevity in the NS- Pten KO mice. There is recent evidence that vitamin D can stimulate expression of DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4), which is a strong suppressor of mTOR activity [ 43 ]. Our study found that the addition of vitamin D did not affect levels of GFAP, however vitamin D did decrease the level of IBA-1. In addition, we found that although vitamin D did not affect levels of mTOR downstream markers of pS6 or pAKT, it did decrease the amount of total S6 and total AKT in the mice overall. Due to these findings, it may be that vitamin D could be a viable therapy option for epileptic patients in conjunction with current epilepsy drugs or those with treatment resistant epilepsy. The addition of vitamin D has been found to aid in neurological disorders like epilepsy. In pilot studies in humans, one intervention of a vitamin D supplement (ranging from 4,000 IU to 16,000 IU) in people with epilepsy was found to decrease the amount of spontaneous seizures [ 32 ]. Another found that after correcting vitamin D deficiency in epileptic patients (oral dose between 40,000 and 200,000 IU) and then administering a daily oral dose of vitamin D (2,000–2,600 IU) reduced the number of seizures by 40% [ 11 ]. A behavioral case study of a toddler with ASD found a high intramuscular dose of vitamin D (150,000 IU) given monthly, and low dose (400 IU) given daily in a toddler with ASD, showed improvement in behavioral deficits that are often comorbid with epilepsy [ 44 ]. 5. Conclusions Our findings suggest that a diet enriched in vitamin D could provide therapeutic benefit by attenuating epilepsy comorbidities observed in NS- Pten mutant mice. Specifically, the current study found that the addition of a vitamin D enriched diet attenuated activity alterations and reduced anxiety levels in the NS- Pten KO, and in addition, increased sociability in WT male animals. The addition of vitamin D in the NS- Pten KO animals also increased survival, decreased level of IBA-1, total S6 and total AKT. Vitamin D was not able to correct bone abnormalities and female NS- Pten mice tended to have greater differences across groups. There were also several behavioral and molecular differences between male and female mice, finding most behavioral differences between males, and most molecular and morphological differences in females. Males NS- Pten KO tend to have a more severe phenotype than female mice and have a higher attrition rate. Due to the severity of the PTEN mutation, the level of vitamin D may not have been high enough to attenuate all molecular abnormalities, especially in male NS- Pten KO mice. Additionally, epilepsy is more prevalent in males than in females in a clinical population [ 45 ]. This adds to the growing evidence of the necessity of including both sexes in studies. Future research is necessary to aid in our understanding of dietary interventions in relation to sex differences. Another limitation of this study was the wide variation of mobility across behavioral measures. For example, male NS- Pten mice displayed a hypoactive phenotype in the open field test, but a hypoactive phenotype in the elevated plus maze, and an increase in percent freezing in the delayed fear conditioning paradigm compared to WT animals. This variation makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions on the impact, if any, the PTEN mutation has on mobility. Previous studies from our lab have found deficits in motor function of NS- Pten KO animals compared to WT animals across all trials of a rotarod task [ 22 ]. Vitamin D is essential for health, in addition to seizure control for epilepsy [ 11 ] as well as targeting some of the comorbidities associated with epilepsy such as behavioral abnormalities [ 44 ]. This current study adds to the growing evidence of the importance of vitamin D in brain development and function and should be considered as a supplemental therapeutic treatment. Highlights. Vitamin D enriched diet attenuated hypoactivity and rearing in the open field test. Vitamin D enriched diet increased percent survival in male and female NS- Pten KO mice. Vitamin D enriched diet did not reverse bone deficits in the NS- Pten KO animals. Vitamin D enriched diet did not decrease pS6 or pAKT levels compared to mice on the CTL diet.
