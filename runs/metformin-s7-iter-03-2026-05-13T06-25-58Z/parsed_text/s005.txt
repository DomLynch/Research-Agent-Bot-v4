For commercial re-use, please contact journals.permissions@oup.com Abstract Mycoplasma pneumoniae ( M. pneumoniae ) is a major pathogen causing community-acquired pneumonia in children. Its pathogenic process relies on the adherence to and colonization of host respiratory epithelial cells. P1 protein is the primary adhesin of M. pneumoniae , directly mediating its binding to host cells. To explore the interaction mechanism between P1 recombinant protein and host cells, we conducted protein expression and purification, glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay, and transcriptome sequencing. The rP1-GST fusion protein was expressed under confirmed induction conditions (16°C, 0.1 mM IPTG). GST pull-down assay identified 22 differentially expressed membrane proteins in the rP1-GST group, among which annexin A2 (ANXA2) and C-C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) were significantly altered and interact with P1 adhesin. Both ANXA2 and CCR5 possessed multiple functions including protein binding, receptor activity and signal sensor activity. Transcriptome analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes from rP1-A549 cell interaction were significantly enriched in multiple Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways. These results suggest that ANXA2 and CCR5 may serve as potential binding partners of P1 adhesin. P1 adhesin may regulate host genes involved in mitochondria and energy metabolism. These findings provide clues for understanding the adhesion and pathogenesis of M. pneumoniae . Our study uncovers key host interactions of Mycoplasma pneumoniae P1 protein, providing a theoretical basis for clarifying P1 adhesinâ€™s biological function and the molecular mechanism of M. pneumoniae infection. It possesses a relatively small genome, with a size ranging from ∼816 402 to 818,633 bp, and is among the microbes with the smallest known genomes (Xiao et al. 2015 ). As a major pathogen causing community-acquired pneumonia in children, M. pneumoniae exhibits notable epidemiological significance. According to domestic statistical data, the incidence of M. pneumoniae infection in the general population is 1–3 cases per thousand. Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) accounts for 10%–30% of all pneumonia cases, and this proportion can increase to 30%–50% during epidemic peak years, with each major epidemic lasting 1–2 years (Meyer Sauteur et al. 2014 , Jain et al. 2015 , Zhu et al. 2018 ). Mycoplasma pneumoniae spreads via respiratory droplets, causing not only respiratory infection but also extrapulmonary complications (such as erythra, meningoencephalitis and asthma), and even death (Klement et al. 2006 , de Groot et al. 2017 , Waites et al. 2017 ). Mycoplasma pneumoniae colonizes host respiratory mucosa via a unique adhesion organelle to initiate pathogenesis. Its pathogenic mechanism is complex: characterized by direct and indirect damage to the host, as well as the exacerbation of tissue injury through interactions with the host immune system (Miyata and Hamaguchi 2016 , Sun et al. 2025 ). Notably, its core pathogenesis involves four key steps: adhesion/colonization, immune escape, inflammatory injury, and cytotoxicity (Jiang et al. 2021 ). Immune escape is critical for its survival and infection progression. Insufficient host immunity allows uncontrolled M. pneumoniae proliferation, worsening tissue damage (Sfriso et al. 2010 , Saraya et al. 2014 ). Immune escape occurs via mechanisms like antigen mimicry of host components (inducing autoimmunity and multi-organ injury) and binding of virulence factor IbpM to host immunoglobulins (Blötz et al. 2020 ). Furthermore, M. pneumoniae releases self-components, metabolites, and toxins to induce inflammatory injury. For example, virulence factor HapE mediates inflammation via hydrogen sulfide production; MPN133-encoded nuclease induces apoptosis; and its lipoproteins bind TLR1/2/6, promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine release via the NF-κB pathway (Into et al. 2004 , 2007 , Kang et al. 2015 , Li et al. 2019 ). Community acquired respiratory distress syndrome toxin, a unique ADP-ribosylating toxin, binds respiratory epithelial surface proteins, undergoes endocytosis, activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, enhances pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and induces allergic inflammation (Kannan and Baseman 2006 , Medina et al. 2012 , Becker et al. 2015 , Maselli et al. 2018 , Saber et al. 2020 ). Crucially, adhesion initiates M. pneumoniae infection. It induces host cytoskeletal rearrangement to facilitate intracellular delivery. After binding cells tightly, M. pneumoniae seizes nutrients, releases damaging metabolites, and triggers immune responses and multi-organ lesions. Thus, adhesin research is key to identify its pathogenicity (Miyata 2010 ). The apical adhesion organelle of M. pneumoniae is a complex structure containing adhesins, chaperone molecules, and accessory proteins, with P1 and P30 as core adhesins (Chaudhry et al. 2007 ). Specifically, P1 adhesin is located at the top of the outer membrane of M. pneumoniae and is encoded by ∼5000 bp (1627 amino acids) with antigenicity and immunogenicity. Studies have confirmed M. pneumoniae loses adhesive ability if P1 is deficient or mis-localized (Seto and Miyata 2003 , Nakane et al. 2021 ). Functional assays show anti-P1 monoclonal antibodies interfere with host cell adhesion, inhibit sliding motility, and fully abrogate wild-type M. pneumoniae adhesion (Dumke and Jacobs 2016 , Vizarraga et al. 2020 ). Moreover, P1-specific antibodies are detectable in infected patients’ sera, confirming P1’s key role in adhesion (Zhu et al. 2012 ). This study aims to identify of P1-interacting proteins by glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down and analysis of its transcriptomic regulation in A549 cells. Materials and methods Construction of the recombinant expression plasmid Mycoplasma Pneumoniae reference strain M129 (ATCC 29342) was cultured in pleuro-pneumonia-like organisms (PPLO) medium (Difo, Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD). Genome DNA was extracted from 400 μl of each sample using TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd. Beijing, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A partial fragment of the p1 gene (3696–4208 bp, GenBank accession no. M18639.1 ), which harbors the epitope region of the P1 protein and was verified in our previous study to exhibit higher immunogenicity in the C-terminal domain relative to the N-terminal domain, was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The gene-specific primers used were as follows: forward primer 5'-AAGGGGGTGTGGGCGGATCAA-3' and reverse primer 5'-GGTGGAGGAGGTGTTTCC-3' (Xue et al. 2013 ). Subsequent to amplification, the PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing, with the obtained sequences further validated by BLAST analysis on the NCBI database ( http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi ). The verified p1 gene fragment was then double-digested with XhoI and BamHI restriction endonucleases, in accordance with the sequence of the cloned region. The resulting digested product was purified and ligated into the pGEX-6P-1 expression vector fused with an N-terminal GST tag. pGEX-6P-1 had been pre-digested with the same pair of restriction enzymes. Expression and purification of GST tag P1 recombinant protein Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS cells carrying recombinant pGEX-6P-1 plasmid were cultured at 37°C. Until the culture density reached an OD600 value of 0.6–0.8, the high levels of expression of P1 recombinant protein (rP1-GST) were obtained upon induction with isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C; the supernatant was discarded. The bacterial pellet was resuspended with lysis buffer containing 1 mM DTT and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and sonicated. Then, the cell lysates were centrifugated for 60 min at 4°C. The supernatant was subjected to SDS-PAGE to detect the expression of the fusion protein. Glutathione Sepharose 4B Resin (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) was used for affinity purification of GST–tagged P1 recombinant protein, and the purified protein was identified by Western blot. GST pull-down assay and LC-MS/MS analysis Human lung A549 cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, then lysed on ice for 3 min with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. After gentle resuspension, cell lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to collect total protein extraction. For GST pull-down assays, recombinant protein rP1-GST was immobilized on Glutathione Sepharose 4B Resin. The A549 cell lysates were then added and incubated with rP1-GST for 1 h. Then, the resin was washed three times with PBS. Bound proteins were eluted using 0.5 ml of glutathione elution buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and 10 mM reduced glutathione. Pull-down products were separated by SDS-PAGE. Electrophoretic patterns of rP1-GST and GST control groups (parallel groups using purified GST tag protein without P1 fragment, to exclude non-specific protein binding to the GST tag) were compared to identify differential bands. Target bands were excised and subjected to digest. The digested peptides were analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and the resulting peptide data were searched against databases to identify candidate proteins interacting with rP1. rP1 adhesin interacts with A549 cells and transcriptome analysis Human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells were seeded in cell culture dishes and cultured under standard conditions until they reached 70%–80% confluence. Cells were treated with 5 μM (230 μg/ml) rP1–GST and an equal concentration of GST protein for 24 h. Following treatment, total RNA was extracted from the cells using TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, Liaoning, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a spectrophotometer. RNA quantity and integrity were determined using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were used for subsequent library construction DNA contamination was analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and qPCR performed in parallel without reverse transcription. Transcriptome sequencing was performed by Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China) on the Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled using fastp (v0.19.7). Clean data (reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapter sequences, N base, and low-quality reads. The total number of raw reads and clean reads for each sample are summarized in Table S3 . All the downstream analysis were performed based on the clean data. Gene expression was quantified using featureCounts (v1.5.0-p3) (Details are shown in Supplementary Materials Table S3 ). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed. Statistical analysis rP1-GST protein expression was quantified by ImageJ software for SDS-PAGE gel analysis. For GST pull-down assays, high-quality peptides were filtered and aligned with the Uniprot database to identify candidate interacting proteins. High-quality peptides were defined with a confidence score ≥ 95% (FDR &lt; 1%) and aligned to the Uniprot human database. Subsequently, these proteins were analyzed via protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using the STRING human database (Details are shown in Supplementary Materials Fig. S6 ). For transcriptome sequencing, raw reads were filtered to obtain clean reads, and differential expressed genes (DEGs) were screened with |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and padj &lt; 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to annotate the functions and signaling pathways of DEGs, and the association between interacting proteins and DEGs was analyzed. All raw transcriptome sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1451183. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). DESeq2 provides statistical routines in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s (BH) approach to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Results Expression and purification of recombinant GST tag P1 adhesin To obtain the target P1 protein for subsequent experiments, a specific fragment of the P1 gene with a length of 513 bp was selected as the expression template. This 513-bp sequence is located at the C-terminal region of the full-length P1 gene, and preliminary experimental verification confirmed that this region has good immunogenicity. In order to facilitate the purification of the expressed P1 protein and meet the experimental requirements of the GST pull-down assay (to screen and identify proteins interacting with P1), we cloned this 513-bp gene fragment into the pGEX-6P-1 expression vector carrying a GST tag, enabling the recombinant vector to express the rP1-GST fusion protein. The GST tag is used for affinity purification of the fusion protein, and the P1 protein fragment is the core target for subsequent functional experiments. The expression level of the fusion protein varies under different temperatures and IPTG concentrations. To optimize the induction conditions for rP1-GST protein expression, we adopted different induction temperatures, IPTG concentrations, and induction durations, the expression levels of the protein was detected and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The results showed that the highest expression level was observed at 16°C, followed by 25°C, 20°C, and 37°C; rP1-GST protein exhibited the highest expression level when induced with 0.1 mM IPTG, followed by 0.2 mM, 0.5 mM, and 1.0 mM IPTG. In contrast, the induction duration had no obvious difference in the expression level when induced for 4 h, 5 h, 8 h, 12 h, and 24 h, respectively. So, the protein expression was induced with the optimized induction conditions (OD₆₀₀ value of bacterial culture reaching 0.6, induction temperature of 16 °C) and addition of IPTG at a final concentration of 0.1 mM, so as to promote the efficient expression of the rP1-GST protein in BL21 cells (Details are shown in Supplementary Materials Fig. S1-S4 ). The molecular weight of the rP1 protein is 20 kDa, while the molecular weight of the GST tag is 26 kDa; therefore, the molecular weight of the rP1-GST protein is approximately 46 kDa, which can be identified by Western blot. Discussion The adhesion of M. pneumoniae to host respiratory epithelial cells depends on multiple adhesin proteins, including P1, P30, and P116. Among them, the P1 protein is one of the major adhesins and immunogenic proteins on the attachment organelle of M. pneumoniae (Peng et al. 2023 ). The P1 protein has attracted attention since the late 1970s. Initially, it was hypothesized that P1 adhesin mediates pathogen adhesion by binding to sialylated oligosaccharide receptors on host cells. Subsequent studies confirmed that adhesive function of P1 adhesin requires the participation of auxiliary proteins (Hu et al. 1982 , Krause et al. 1982 , Kornspan et al. 2011 , Vizarraga et al. 2021 ). Existing evidence has demonstrated that P1 adhesin can form a transmembrane complex named “Nap” together with P40/P90 polypeptides. Both the motility and infectivity of M. pneumoniae are regulated by the transmembrane adhesion complex, which is mediated by the immunodominant protein P1 and P40/P90 (Burgos et al. 2006 , Scheffer et al. 2017 ). In recent years, analysis using X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy has revealed a key finding. The sialic acid-binding site is located in the P40/P90 protein, rather than in the P1 protein (Vizarraga et al. 2020 ). Xue et al. confirmed the C-terminal of M. pneumoniae P1 adhesin is its key functional domain with specific immunogenicity (Xue et al. 2013 ). On this basis, we conducted a further analysis to explore the interaction between P1 adhesin and host airway epithelial cells. This study explored potential interacting proteins of M. pneumoniae P1 adhesin using GST pull-down assay. It also investigated the adhesin’s mechanism of action. To clarify P1 interaction with A549 cell membrane proteins, a prokaryotic expression vector of P1 was constructed. Its expression conditions were optimized (16°C induction with 0.1 mM IPTG for 6 h). Notably, low concentration IPTG can moderately activate the T7 promoter of the pGEX-6P-1 vector, avoiding excessive metabolic pressure on bacterial cells and abnormal protein folding caused by high concentration induction. Meanwhile, the low temperature of 16°C can reduce the degradation of the target protein by proteases and decrease the probability of inclusion body formation. After purifying the rP1-GST fusion protein, it was used as the bait protein to perform a pull-down assay with total A549 cell proteins. Mass spectrometry identification results showed that a total of 72 proteins interacting with rP1-GST were identified. Among these, 22 were localized to the cell membrane. The most significantly differential ones were CCR5 and ANXA2. Both CCR5 and ANXA2 possessed multiple functions, including catalytic activity, metal ion binding, protein binding, receptor activity, and signal sensor activity. This result suggests that the P1 adhesin, as a key virulence-related protein of M. pneumoniae , may mediate the adhesion, invasion, and signal transduction processes between pathogens and host cells by targeting specific proteins on the host cell membrane. To further explore the potential functional interplay among the identified P1-interacting membrane proteins, we conducted a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis via the STRING database. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S6 , the network revealed a densely connected core module. This module was mainly composed of three categories of proteins: cytoskeletal proteins (for example, ACTB, GFAP), chaperone proteins (for instance, HSPA5, HSPA8), and translation-associated factors (for instance, EEF1A1, EEF2). These findings may indicate that M. pneumoniae P1 adhesin has the potential to disrupt the host cytoskeleton and protein synthesis machinery. This disruption could contribute to bacterial adhesion and invasion of the bacterium into host cells. Furthermore, CCR5 and other membrane proteins were identified as peripheral nodes in the network. This suggests they may act as specific receptors or regulatory molecules during M. pneumoniae infection. CCR5 is a G protein-coupled receptor. It is mainly expressed in T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, eosinophils, microglia, and subsets of breast or prostate cancer cells. A 32 bp deletion mutation in the CCR5 gene (CCR5△32) may be associated with asthma susceptibility. This study is the first to find that it may interact with M. pneumoniae , suggesting that it may be hijacked by the P1 adhesin to inhibit the host innate immune response, thereby facilitating the immune escape of M. pneumoniae . ANXA2 is a calcium-dependent phospholipid-binding protein. It is involved in cell movement, endocytosis, and the adhesion process of multiple pathogens. It can localize to the cell membrane as monomers or heterotetramers. Somarajan SR et al. found that ANXA2 can act as a receptor to assist the internalization of the CARDS toxin of M. pneumoniae (Somarajan et al. 2014 ); Chengchao Shou et al. pointed out that anti-ANXA2 protein antibodies can block the binding of Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae lipoprotein P37 to cells and cell infiltration (Duan et al. 2014 ); ANXA2 also can act as a recognition site and anchor protein for viruses and bacteria on the cell surface (Ryzhova et al. 2006 ). ANXA2 promotes human papillomavirus subtype 16 (HPV16) infection and interacts with HPV16 (Dziduszko and Ozbun 2013 ); The infection process of Pseudomonas aeruginosa is also related to ANXA2 (Kirschnek et al. 2005 ). The interaction between ANXA2 and the rP1 protein in this study further confirms its role in pathogen-host interactions, and it is speculated that this interaction may be involved in the adhesion and invasion of A549 cells by M. pneumoniae . The results of transcriptome GO enrichment analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes after the interaction between the P1 recombinant protein and A549 cells were significantly enriched in mitochondrial components, ribosomes, and myofilament sliding. Furthermore, after the interaction between the P1 protein and A549 cells, it may target and regulate the mitochondrial function of host cells by damaging the integrity of the mitochondrial inner membrane, affecting the energy metabolism of host cells, thereby inhibiting the normal physiological functions of host cells and creating conditions for the invasion and survival of M. pneumoniae . KEGG signaling pathway enrichment analysis found that the HRAS gene is enriched in inflammation-related signaling pathways such as RAS/MAPK. The H-Ras protein encoded by HRAS may regulate cell proliferation and differentiation by activating the RAF-MEK-ERK pathway. It also collaborates with the pro-apoptotic gene BAD to induce host cell apoptosis, jointly participating in the pathogenic process of M. pneumoniae (Gripp and Lin 2012 ). This study provides the first evidence for the potential role of the P1 protein in M. pneumoniae infection. The P1 protein may mediate M. pneumoniae adhesion to host cells via CCR5 and ANXA2. It also appears to be involved in the regulation of inflammation-related pathways. This provides a new perspective for analyzing the pathogenic mechanism of M. pneumoniae . However, this study has some limitations. First, rP1-GST purification shows impurity bands. The result may be due to insufficient protease inhibition. A long elution time leading to protein degradation may be another possible reason. These may interfere with subsequent analyses. Besides, the direct interaction between P1 and ANXA2/CCR5 has not been fully verified. In the future, we will optimize the purification protocol to obtain high-purity rP1-GST. We will also use Co-IP and immunofluorescence to verify the specificity of protein interactions. RNA interference and overexpression can be used to explore the functions of differentially expressed genes and interacting proteins. In addition, we will further explore P1-induced inflammation in host cells. The transcriptome data showed DEGs enriched in mitochondrial pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and ROS metabolism. Mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to P1-triggered inflammation. We will detect mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS, and ATP levels, as well as the expression of mitochondrial respiratory complexes. This will verify the impact of P1 protein on mitochondrial function based on transcriptome data. Conclusion Mycoplasma pneumoniae P1 adhesin can interact with proteins such as CCR5 and ANXA2 on the membrane of host A549 cells. CCR5 and ANXA2 have functions including catalytic activity, metal ion binding, protein binding, receptor activity, and signal sensor activity. P1 adhesin regulates the expression of differentially genes related to host cell mitochondrial function, ribosome function and other pathways. This effect mediates the interaction between pathogens and hosts. This finding reveals a new mechanism of the P1 adhesin in M. pneumoniae infection. This finding supplements the deficiencies of current studies on P1 protein and host cell interaction.
