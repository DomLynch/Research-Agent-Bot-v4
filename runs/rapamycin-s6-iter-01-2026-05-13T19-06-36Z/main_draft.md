## Discussion

The central question of this scoping review was to map and characterize the evidence base on rapamycin (sirolimus) administration and murine lifespan. The analysis indicates that the current automated pipeline identified a set of contract-passing primary studies for inclusion. However, the number of effect estimates meeting the universal evidence-contract gate within a single metric family is small. Therefore, the corpus provides narrative but not pooled quantitative support for the hypothesis that rapamycin intervention alters murine lifespan. Any interpretation of effect size or consistency from the primary corpus must be tempered by this low study count.

Prior quantitative syntheses have examined rapamycin and lifespan across murine models, often concluding a positive effect on longevity [CIT:fok-2023-meta-rapamycin|prior-meta-analysis] [CIT:swindell-2017-meta|prior-meta-analysis]. A more recent cross-vertebrate meta-analysis also includes murine data [CIT:tyshkovskiy-2025-vertebrate-meta|prior-meta-analysis]. The present work extends this literature by applying a strict, pipeline-enforced evidence contract to isolate a primary corpus for potential future synthesis, ensuring that included studies explicitly report on the declared endpoint and comparator. The alignment with prior syntheses regarding the direction of a lifespan effect cannot be formally tested here due to the small k, but the narrative pattern across the contracted corpus is broadly consistent with the positive signal reported in larger pooled analyses. Divergence, if present, may stem from this pipeline's stricter study-level reporting thresholds for automated extraction.

The mechanistic rationale for rapamycin's potential geroprotective effects centers on its inhibition of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. This pathway integrates nutrient and growth factor signals to regulate cellular growth, proliferation, and metabolism [CIT:lamming-2013-mtor|mechanism-review] [CIT:saxton-2017-mtor|mechanism-review]. Inhibition of mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) is posited to mimic aspects of dietary restriction, enhancing cellular stress resistance and metabolic homeostasis. It is important to frame this mechanism background not as a finding of the present review, but as the established biological context that motivates the experimental interventions captured in the primary corpus.

Boundary conditions that could moderate rapamycin's effect on lifespan are currently underdetermined in the contracted corpus due to limited study numbers. Factors such as murine genetic background, sex, administered dose, timing of intervention initiation (lifelong vs. late-life), route of administration, and specific pathogen status of the animal facilities are reported variably across studies. While prior narrative reviews discuss these potential moderators [CIT:arriola-apelo-2016-review|narrative-review], the present scoping review cannot quantify their influence. The pipeline is designed for moderator analysis via mixed-effects meta-regression, but this capability is deferred until a larger corpus of contract-passing effects accumulates.

Translational considerations are informed by clinical trials in humans, which have explored everolimus and the catalytic-site inhibitor RTB101, both rapamycin analogs, for effects on immune function and infection susceptibility in older adults [CIT:mannick-2014-everolimus|clinical-trial] [CIT:mannick-2018-rtb101|clinical-trial]. These studies provide a human pharmacodynamic context but are explicitly excluded from the primary murine-lifespan corpus under the defined intervention eligibility rules. The translational sensitivity layer of the pipeline permits their separate discussion as evidence for mTOR pathway modulation in aging biology, but they do not inform the direct efficacy question regarding murine lifespan extension by rapamycin itself.

## Limitations

This analysis is subject to several specific constraints inherent to its automated pipeline and the current evidence landscape. The primary corpus size, defined by the number of studies passing the universal evidence-contract gate, is low, which severely limits statistical inference and precludes meta-analytic pooling within metric families. The sentinel-recall gate, which assesses the pipeline's ability to recover known landmark studies, is reported via [PACKET:sentinel_recall]; incomplete recall would indicate a potential retrieval or eligibility gap in the automated sweep. Furthermore, the pipeline's strict metric-family discipline—pooling only within families such as 90th-percentile ratios or median ratios, but not across them—means that effects reported in different lifespan metrics cannot be jointly modeled. Risk-of-bias assessment is conducted via automated LLM adjudication without explicit human arbitration, a process that may not capture nuanced methodological concerns identified by domain expertise using an appropriate framework, which is deferred. Finally, any instances of manual full-text injection for sentinels the auto-retrieval could not reach, as documented in [PACKET:study_selection], represent source recovery rather than a change to the predefined eligibility criteria, and do not alter the fundamental small-k limitation of the primary evidence set.

## Conclusion

The current evidence corpus mapped by this automated pipeline supports a narrative pattern consistent with prior reports that rapamycin administration can extend murine lifespan, but the number of contract-passing effect estimates is insufficient for a pooled quantitative synthesis. A stronger conclusion would require a larger set of primary studies meeting the automated evidence contract, or successful sentinel-study recovery to enrich the corpus. The interpretive frame remains constrained by the underdetermined influence of key methodological boundary conditions within this primary evidence set.
