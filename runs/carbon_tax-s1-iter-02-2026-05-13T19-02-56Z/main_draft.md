# Systematic Synthesis of Carbon Tax Effects on Emissions, Output, and Competitiveness

## Abstract

To what extent do carbon taxes consistently reduce national or sectoral greenhouse gas emissions, and what key factors moderate this relationship? Existing systematic reviews [CIT:Metcalfe2019carbon] and meta-analyses [CIT:Hassett2020carbon] have synthesized empirical evidence but identified substantial heterogeneity linked to study context, policy design, and outcome measurement. This open question motivates a reproducible evidence-contract pipeline to evaluate the consistency and moderators of carbon tax efficacy. The pipeline implements a multi-database retrieval sweep, followed by eligibility adjudication using a deterministic rule-triage pre-pass and an LLM judge applying a universal evidence-contract gate. The screening process yields [N_SCREENED] initial records, anticipated to result in [N_ACCEPTED] contract-passing studies comprising [K_STUDIES] independent effect estimates for analysis.

The planned analysis involves inverse-variance random-effects pooling for contract-passing effects within a single metric family at k≥2. Mixed-effects models are specified to partition heterogeneity and test pre-specified moderators, including stringency (e.g., tax level, coverage), jurisdictional economic structure, complementary policies, and study method (e.g., difference-in-differences vs. computable general equilibrium). A tension matrix mapping effect direction and magnitude against moderator strata is a designed-for capability, deferred until the corpus accrues sufficient studies. The analysis is designed to estimate whether the effect of a carbon tax on emissions is conditional on its interaction with energy market structure, pre-existing regulations, and the timeframe of outcome measurement.

The resulting synthesis is designed to map the empirical landscape, quantify the central tendency and dispersion of effects, and identify where evidence is robust versus contested. The findings are intended to inform the design of cost-effective climate policy by highlighting design features that enhance environmental effectiveness while potentially mitigating negative economic competitiveness effects. Strengthening the evidence base requires future primary studies employing quasi-experimental designs that better isolate causal pathways and measure longer-term dynamic responses.

## Introduction

Climate change mitigation policies are a critical area of empirical economics and environmental policy research. A carbon tax is frequently advocated as a market-based instrument to internalize the externality of greenhouse gas emissions, incentivizing a shift toward lower-carbon technologies and practices. The theoretical efficiency of such a tax is well-established, yet its empirical effectiveness—measured as the causal reduction in emissions—and its broader economic impacts are subject to considerable debate and observed variation across implementations. Understanding the magnitude and consistency of these effects, and the factors that explain differences in outcomes, is essential for evidence-based policy design and international climate cooperation.

The academic literature on the impacts of carbon taxes has grown substantially, encompassing evaluations of specific national or sub-national policies, cross-country comparative studies, and theoretical simulations. Prior syntheses have sought to aggregate this evidence. Metcalfe and Hsu [CIT:Metcalfe2019carbon] conducted a systematic review that catalogued empirical findings, highlighting a general trend of emissions reductions but noting the influence of policy design and context. Subsequent meta-analytic work by Hassett and colleagues [CIT:Hassett2020carbon] attempted a quantitative synthesis, estimating an average effect but encountering high heterogeneity (I² > 80%), which limited the strength of pooled conclusions. These foundational syntheses confirmed that carbon taxes can reduce emissions but also made clear that the "average effect" is a poor summary, as outcomes appear highly contingent.

Existing syntheses, while invaluable, leave specific gaps that this work aims to address. First, prior quantitative meta-analyses have often struggled with the methodological diversity of primary studies, mixing structural model estimates with reduced-form econometric evaluations, which may introduce unexamined heterogeneity. Second, the moderating role of complementary policies—such as renewable energy subsidies or energy efficiency standards—interacting with the carbon tax has not been systematically quantified in a multi-level framework. Third, the potential for carbon leakage and competitiveness effects, a major political concern, has been examined in individual studies but not synthesized across the literature to identify consistent conditional relationships. This paper does not claim to be the first synthesis on carbon taxes, but rather aims to extend and refine the quantitative evidence base by applying a stringent evidence-contract pipeline designed to enhance internal validity and explicitly model sources of heterogeneity.

The contribution of this research is a stress-test of the carbon tax literature's robustness through a reproducible, transparent analytical pipeline. Unlike some prior reviews, this synthesis employs a universal evidence-contract gate requiring parsed-text adequacy and endpoint coverage, ensuring that included studies provide direct evidence on the relationship of interest. It moves beyond simple pooling by implementing a pre-specified mixed-effects modeling framework to simultaneously test multiple moderator hypotheses. The analysis is designed to map effect heterogeneity by policy design (tax level and revenue recycling), jurisdictional context (GDP per capita, energy intensity), and study characteristics (methodology, outcome measurement period). This approach allows for a more nuanced estimation of under what conditions carbon taxes are likely to be most environmentally effective and economically neutral.

The scope of this synthesis is explicitly bounded to empirical evaluations of implemented carbon pricing policies, focusing on ex-post analyses of their effects on emissions and macroeconomic or sectoral competitiveness indicators. It excludes purely theoretical simulations or cost-effectiveness studies not grounded in observed data. Non-goals include providing a comprehensive legal or political analysis of carbon tax adoption, conducting a full risk-of-bias assessment using a specific domain framework (this is a designed-for capability deferred for future iterations with larger k), or executing publication-bias diagnostics at this stage. The primary objective is to produce a quantitative summary of effect heterogeneity based on the best available causal and quasi-causal evidence, providing a clearer empirical foundation for policy and future research.
