Abstract Leptin and parathyroid hormone (PTH) are key regulators of metabolic and mineral homeostasis. Leptin, primarily secreted by adipose tissue, controls appetite and energy expenditure through its receptors in the central nervous system, while PTH maintains serum calcium and phosphate balance and skeletal integrity by acting on kidneys and bone. The calcium sensing receptor (CaSR) plays a central role in parathyroid function and PTH secretion. While clinical and experimental evidence suggests reciprocal interactions between leptin and PTH, where hyperleptinemia and disrupted leptin signaling in obesity may exacerbate conditions such as secondary hyperparathyroidism, direct effects of leptin on the parathyroid remain poorly defined. We now show that leptin receptor‐deficient db/db mice exhibit reduced serum PTH levels at 4 and 7 months of age. Complementary ex vivo experiments in cultured mouse parathyroid glands demonstrate that recombinant leptin increases PTH secretion while downregulating CaSR and c‐fos gene expression. Furthermore, CaSR activation using a calcimimetic drug attenuated leptin's stimulatory effect on PTH secretion, indicating that leptin enhances PTH release by down regulating CaSR activity. These findings establish a direct regulatory link between leptin and PTH, highlighting leptin's role in modulating CaSR activity in the parathyroid. By counteracting CaSR‐mediated inhibition, leptin may prevent excessive suppression of PTH release by the CaSR. Clinically, these insights may hold implications for conditions such as hyperleptinemia, obesity, and other disorders of PTH dysregulation. Interactions between adipose tissue, parathyroid glands, and kidneys mediated by leptin, PTH, and 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 . Leptin, predominantly secreted by adipocytes, directly stimulates PTH secretion by inhibiting the CasR/pERK1/2 signaling, lifting CasR‐mediated suppression of PTH secretion. Elevated PTH may affect leptin production. The parathyroid glands themselves produce leptin, further integrating these pathways. PTH stimulates renal 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 (1,25D) synthesis, which suppresses PTH and regulates calcium and phosphate homeostasis. Leptin reduces 1,25D, indirectly promoting PTH secretion by decreasing the inhibitory feedback exerted by 1,25D on the parathyroid. This network links leptin to mineral regulation via PTH and 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 . Danielle Melloul and Tally Naveh‐Many equal contributing corresponding authors. 1 INTRODUCTION Parathyroid hormone (PTH) maintains serum calcium and phosphate levels within a narrow physiological range, contributing to bone health and overall mineral homeostasis. PTH exerts its effects by binding to the PTH receptor 1 (PTHR1) in target tissues. In bone, PTH promotes calcium release through osteoclast stimulation, while in the kidney it enhances calcium reabsorption and facilitates phosphate excretion. In addition, PTH indirectly increases intestinal calcium absorption by stimulating the production of active vitamin D (1,25(OH) 2 D 3, 1,25D). 1,25D in turn inhibits PTH synthesis and secretion through a negative feedback mechanism. The regulation of PTH secretion is primarily controlled by the calcium sensing receptor (CaSR), a G‐protein‐coupled receptor highly expressed in parathyroid glands. 1 Activation of the CaSR by extracellular calcium or pharmacological agents like calcimimetics decreases PTH gene expression, secretion, and parathyroid cell proliferation via downstream signaling pathways. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 A low extracellular calcium has opposite effects. Leptin mainly secreted by white adipose tissue, is a key regulator of appetite and energy balance. It exerts its effects through specific leptin receptors in the central nervous system influencing satiety and energy expenditure. Beyond its roles in metabolic body mass regulation, leptin affects various physiologic processes, including the hypothalamic–pituitary axis, gonadal function, immune responses, and bone metabolism. 6 Recent evidence suggests potential crosstalk between leptin and PTH, linking energy metabolism with mineral homeostasis and skeletal health. 7 Clinical observations have demonstrated increased leptin levels in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism, although not consistently observed across all studies. 8 , 9 In some cases, surgical removal of the parathyroid glands in these patients has been associated with reduced serum leptin levels, implying a role for PTH in leptin production. 10 Conversely, elevated PTH levels are commonly observed in obesity, potentially attributed to increased circulating leptin levels. Initial studies indicated that leptin's effects on PTH secretion were indirect, mediated by the upregulation of the bone‐produced phosphaturic hormone fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) and a reduction in 1,25D, both known to influence PTH levels. 7 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Subsequent studies have proposed an additional direct stimulatory effect of leptin on PTH secretion, even in the absence of significant changes in 1,25D and FGF23 expression. 16 The parathyroid glands express leptin and its receptor, as measured in patients with hyperparathyroidism as well as in normal parathyroid glands. The detection of leptin expression in parathyroid glands, particularly in hyperplasia, indicates that the parathyroid gland may be a source of leptin production. 17 Specific leptin receptor polymorphisms have been associated with parathyroid gland size, indicating that genetic variations in the leptin pathway may predispose individuals to hyperparathyroidism. 10 Experimental studies in rodents have provided further insights into the leptin‐PTH relationship. Leptin administration increased PTH levels in leptin‐deficient ( ob/ob ) and control ob/+ heterozygous mice. Despite a limited sample size (three ob/ob mice and one ob/+ heterozygous control), these studies suggested a direct effect of leptin on PTH secretion in leptin‐deficient states. 17 db/db mice, which carry an inactivating mutation in the leptin receptor causing non‐insulin‐dependent diabetes mellitus, exhibit reduced serum 1,25D levels and a mild increase in serum PTH at 12 weeks of age but not at other time points. The transient elevation in PTH was attributed to a decline in 1,25D levels 18 ; however, the reliability of these findings may have been affected by limitations in mouse serum PTH measurements available at the time these experiments were conducted. This study also reported reduced bone mineral density in db/db mice from 12 weeks of age, which was ascribed to insufficient vitamin D activity and elevated PTH levels, contributing to osteopenia. 18 Additionally, another study found that db/db mice exhibited increased FGF23 and phosphate levels. 19 Streptozotocin‐induced diabetic rats showed reduced PTH and 1,25D levels alongside impaired bone turnover. 20 In leptin receptor‐deficient Zucker rats, basal serum PTH levels were comparable to those of controls, and leptin administration increased PTH levels in wild‐type but not in leptin receptor‐deficient rats, confirming that intact leptin receptor signaling is essential for leptin's effect on PTH secretion. 16 Furthermore, human recombinant leptin increased PTH secretion in hyperplastic parathyroid gland explants, while leptin receptor inhibition reduced secretion, emphasizing leptin receptor‐mediated regulation of PTH. 17 Despite growing evidence linking leptin and PTH, the role of leptin receptor signaling in parathyroid glands and the mechanisms by which leptin regulates PTH secretion remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate the leptin‐PTH relationship, using both in vivo and ex vivo approaches in db/db and control mice. Understanding this relationship could have significant clinical implications, particularly for managing obesity and hyperleptinemia in patients with hyperparathyroidism. 2 METHODS 2.1 Animal housing All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Hebrew University, Jerusalem. All mice had free access to food and water. 2.2 db/− , db/db mice db/− , db/db , and wild‐type mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine). Male mice were selected based on genotype. Mice were analyzed at 4 and 7 months of age. Animal group sizes were decided based on our previous experience with biochemical variance between individual animals. No subjects were excluded due to attrition or dropout during the study period. Researchers were not blinded to animal genotypes. 2.3 Parathyroid‐specific PT ‐ TdTomato mice Parathyroid‐specific tdTomato (PT‐ TdTomato ) expressing mice, generated using parathyroid‐specific PTH‐ Cre mediated recombination and a loxP‐ stop cassette‐controlled tdTomato marker gene, were utilized for fluorescence‐guided microscopy. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and total calcium levels were measured using QuantiChrom kits (BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA). In some experiments, serum calcium levels were measured using the Atellica CH Calcium kit (Tarrytown, NY). Serum PTH and PTH secreted into the growth medium in organ cultures were measured using a mouse 1–84 Intact PTH ELISA kit (Quidel, Athens, OH). 3 2.5 Parathyroid glands in culture Parathyroid glands from PT‐ TdTomato mice were microdissected, and the 2 glands from individual mice were placed in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes with needle‐puncture caps containing 1 mL of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham MA, RRID:41965039) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco Life Technologies, Waltham, MA, RRID:A5256701), L‐glutamine (Gibco Life Technologies, Waltham, MA, RRID:25030024), and penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco Life Technologies, Waltham, MA). 22 , 23 Recombinant Human leptin Protein (R&amp;D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, RRID:398-LP) at a concentration of 1 μg/mL or vehicle (Tris 20 mM, pH 8) and the calcimimetic R568 (a generous gift from Amgen, Thousand Oaks, CA), (dissolved in DDW) at a final concentration of 1 μM were added to the culture medium. The tubes were maintained in a CO 2 incubator at 37°C under constant rocking to ensure proper mixing and oxygenation of the culture medium. After fixation, the glands were embedded in paraffin, and 6 μm‐thick tissue sections were prepared for analysis. 21 , 22 Antigen retrieval was performed using a pressure cooker at 121°C for 3 min in 20 mM citric acid buffer (pH 6). Differences between two groups were assessed using a two‐tailed Student's t ‐tests, with p values less than .05 considered statistically significant. 3 RESULTS 3.1 Reduced serum PTH in db/db mice at 4 and 7 months with no changes in PTH and calcium sensing receptor mRNA levels To study the impact of whole‐body leptin receptor deficiency on parathyroid function, we measured serum PTH in wild‐type, heterozygous db/− , and leptin receptor‐deficient db/db homozygous mice. Baseline serum PTH levels were comparable between wild‐type and db/− mice (Figure S1 ), validating the use of non‐diabetic db/− mice as the control group for subsequent comparisons. 25 Serum PTH levels were significantly lower in db/db mice compared to db/− mice at both 4 and 7 months of age (Figure 1A,B ). Consistent with reduced PTH levels, serum calcium was decreased at 7 months but remained unchanged at 4 months of age. Serum BUN levels showed no difference at either time point (Figures 1C,D and S2 ). Despite the significant reduction in serum PTH, PTH and CaSR mRNA levels in the thyroparathyroid glands did not differ between db/− and db/db mice (Figure 1E,F ), suggesting that the lower serum PTH levels in db/db mice are not due to altered transcription but rather post‐transcriptional mechanisms affecting PTH protein synthesis, secretion, or stability. PTH protein content was significantly reduced in parathyroid glands of db/db mice compared to db/− controls (Figure 2A,B ). This reduction in PTH protein, despite unchanged PTH mRNA levels (Figure 1E,F ), further supports the involvement of post‐transcriptional mechanisms in the regulation of PTH levels in db/db mice. In contrast, CaSR levels remained unchanged between groups (Figure 2C,D ). Similarly, levels of the FGF23 co‐receptor Klotho were unaffected (Figure 2C,E ), whereas FGFR1 expression was reduced (Figure 2F,G ). Given that db/db mice exhibit elevated circulating FGF23 levels, which are known to suppress PTH secretion, 13 , 15 , 19 , 26 the concurrent decrease in FGFR1 expression may attenuate FGF23‐mediated PTH suppression, 27 potentially serving as a compensatory mechanism in these mice. Quantification was performed using Image J quantification program. * p &lt; .05. 3.3 Leptin increases PTH secretion in cultured parathyroid glands while decreasing CasR levels To study the direct effects of leptin on parathyroid function, we utilized an organ culture system with parathyroid glands isolated from control mice expressing the leptin receptor and parathyroid‐specific tdTomato to facilitate precise visualization and excision of the glands by fluorescence‐guided microscopy 3 , 15 (Figure 3A ). The glands were cultured in growth medium supplemented with recombinant leptin (1 μg/mL) or vehicle. 16 After 3 h of incubation, PTH levels in the culture medium were significantly higher in leptin‐treated glands compared to controls (Figure 3B ). qRT‐PCR analysis of RNA extracted from the glands after 24 h revealed a reduction in CaSR mRNA levels with no change in PTH mRNA levels (Figure 3C ). Given that c‐fos is a downstream target of CaSR and ERK1/2 activation, and that CaSR stimulation upregulates c‐fos promoter activity and expression, 28 we examined c‐fos mRNA levels in leptin treated parathyroids. c‐fos mRNA levels were significantly reduced at 3 h, but not 24 h (not shown), consistent with its role as an early response gene and the observed decrease in CaSR mRNA levels (Figure 3C,D ). These findings indicate that leptin directly stimulates PTH secretion in isolated mouse parathyroid glands and suggest that this effect is through post‐transcriptional regulation mediated by the CaSR. The reduction in CaSR and its downstream target, c‐ fos , likely contributes to this effect by alleviating CaSR‐mediated inhibitory signaling on PTH secretion. 29 FIGURE 3 Leptin increases PTH secretion in microdissected parathyroid glands in culture while decreasing CasR and c‐fos gene expression. To determine whether CaSR plays a role in leptin‐mediated increase in PTH secretion, we assessed the effect of the CaSR activator calcimimetic R568 on this regulation. The addition of R568 attenuated leptin's stimulatory effect on PTH secretion, demonstrating the critical role of CaSR in modulating leptin's influence on the parathyroid glands (Figure 3E ). Thus, leptin increases PTH secretion while suppressing CaSR expression in parathyroid organ cultures, an effect that is counteracted by activation of the CaSR with the calcimimetic R568. These findings underscore the key role of CaSR in modulating leptin's effects on parathyroid function. Collectively, our data demonstrate that leptin stimulates PTH secretion by downregulating CaSR signaling. 4 DISCUSSION This study provides novel insights into leptin's direct role in regulating PTH secretion, highlighting the complex interplay between metabolic signals and mineral homeostasis. Utilizing the db/db mouse model, we demonstrate that leptin signaling deficiency leads to significantly reduced serum PTH levels and parathyroid gland PTH content compared to control db/− mice , despite unchanged PTH or CaSR mRNA levels. At 4 months of age, serum calcium levels remained unchanged in db/db mice, suggesting that lower circulating PTH levels may still be sufficient to maintain calcium homeostasis in these mice at earlier time points. However, by 7 months, the reduced serum PTH was accompanied by decreased serum calcium, indicating a potential age‐dependent shift in the compensatory mechanisms regulating calcium balance. Future studies assessing PTH responsiveness to extracellular calcium fluctuations in db/db mice may provide additional insights into their calcium—sensing sensitivity and adaptive regulation. The selective reduction in PTH protein content and secretion, despite stable mRNA levels, suggests that post‐transcriptional mechanisms suppress PTH protein synthesis, stability, and/or secretion in the absence of functional leptin receptors. Additionally, the decrease in serum PTH levels may be influenced by the previously reported decreases in serum 1,25D levels and increase in FGF23, both of which affect PTH mRNA expression, 14 , 15 and may further contribute to the suppression of serum PTH levels 19 , 30 , 31 (Figure 4 ). FIGURE 4 Schematic representation of interactions between adipose tissue, parathyroid glands, and kidneys mediated by leptin, PTH, and 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 . Leptin, predominantly secreted by adipocytes, directly stimulates PTH secretion by inhibiting the CasR/pERK1/2 signaling pathway, thereby lifting the CasR‐mediated suppression of PTH secretion by the parathyroid glands. Elevated PTH levels, in turn, may affect leptin production by adipocytes. 7 , 17 Additionally, the parathyroid glands themselves produce leptin, further integrating these pathways. PTH stimulates renal synthesis of 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 (1,25D) which suppresses PTH secretion and contributes to calcium and phosphate homeostasis. Leptin reduces 1,25D, thus promoting PTH secretion indirectly by decreasing the inhibitory feedback exerted by 1,25D on the parathyroid glands. This intricate regulatory network links leptin to mineral homeostasis through PTH, CaSR, and 1,25D. Our ex vivo experiments with cultured mouse parathyroid glands demonstrate leptin's direct role in stimulating PTH secretion. Leptin increased PTH secretion while reducing CaSR expression in these cultured glands. Activation of CaSR by extracellular calcium or pharmacological agents such as calcimimetics trigger a cascade of downstream intracellular signaling pathways, including MAPK/ERK pathway. Activation of the ERK1/2 pathway can lead to transcriptional upregulation of immediate early genes like c‐fos . 28 We show that c‐fos mRNA levels were decreased in leptin treated parathyroid glands, further supporting leptin's effect to downregulate CaSR expression and activation. Therefore, leptin may alleviate CasR‐mediated inhibitory signaling on PTH release by downregulating CaSR expression. The discrepancy between unchanged CaSR expression in vivo in db/db mice and its downregulation by leptin ex vivo highlights the complex interplay of systemic and local factors in parathyroid regulation. In db/db mice , systemic compensatory mechanisms, including calcium, phosphate, FGF23, and vitamin D, may stabilize CaSR expression despite an altered hormonal environment, reflecting the robustness of systemic calcium homeostasis. In contrast, organ culture isolates the parathyroid gland from systemic influences, allowing leptin's direct suppressive effects on CaSR expression to emerge. This unmasking suggests leptin's potential role in fine‐tuning parathyroid function by modulating CaSR‐mediated PTH regulation. To further investigate the significance of CaSR in leptin‐mediated regulation of PTH, we assessed the direct effect of CaSR activation on PTH secretion using the calcimimetic R568. R568 significantly attenuated leptin‐induced PTH secretion in vitro, supporting CaSR as a key mediator of leptin's effects on PTH. The reduction in CaSR and c‐fos mRNA levels following leptin treatment, coupled with the suppression of PTH secretion in the presence of R568, demonstrates the crucial role of CaSR in this regulatory mechanism. Our findings, therefore, show that leptin modulates CaSR expression, potentially lowering the threshold for PTH release and thereby enhancing PTH secretion independently of extracellular calcium levels. They propose a regulatory axis between leptin and CaSR in controlling PTH secretion, where leptin may normally counteract the inhibitory effects of CaSR to prevent over‐inhibition of PTH secretion. Our findings align with previous reports linking leptin and PTH, including clinical observations of elevated leptin levels in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism and experimental studies. The current study extends these findings by identifying leptin as a direct regulator of PTH secretion, with CaSR playing a central role in this regulation (Figure 4 ). Furthermore, it highlights a regulatory link between leptin and CaSR in controlling PTH secretion, where leptin counteracts the inhibitory effects of CaSR, thereby preventing excessive suppression of PTH release. Understanding the balance between leptin and PTH in both physiological and pathological conditions provides insights into the metabolic‐skeletal crosstalk mediated by these hormones. It could suggest novel therapeutic avenues for managing metabolic and mineral disorders, particularly in individuals with hyperleptinemia, obesity, and dysregulated PTH levels.
