=== TITLE ===
Quantitative Synthesis of Carbon Tax Efficacy on Emission Reductions Across Diverse Economic Jurisdictions

=== ABSTRACT ===
To what extent does the implementation of carbon taxation reduce aggregate greenhouse gas emissions across varying economic scales and regulatory frameworks? Prior syntheses have identified general trends in carbon pricing efficacy [CIT:author-year-general], yet uncertainty remains regarding the interaction between tax rates and sectoral leakage [CIT:author-year-leakage]. This work is implemented through a reproducible evidence-contract pipeline. The pipeline performs a multi-source retrieval sweep, followed by a single LLM-judge eligibility adjudication against a deterministic rule-triage pre-pass. Records must pass a universal evidence-contract gate requiring parsed-text adequacy, a character-count floor, and at least two non-title evidence quotes covering endpoints and interventions. An A-core audit demotes records failing to affirm the declared study system and primary intervention.

The analysis will process [N_SCREENED] records to identify [N_ACCEPTED] eligible papers across [K_STUDIES] distinct jurisdictions. Effect extraction is performed by a single LLM pass per paper, with a Researka Tier-2 canonical-fact cross-check overlay on numeric percent-changes. The planned analysis utilizes an inverse-variance random-effects pool over contract-passing effects within single metric families at k>=2. The pipeline is designed to estimate the variance partitioned by tax-level strata and jurisdictional GDP. The analysis is designed to quantify whether the magnitude of emission reduction is moderated by the presence of revenue-neutral recycling mechanisms or the inclusion of border adjustment taxes. Specifically, does the efficacy of the tax diminish as the proportion of energy-intensive trade-exposed industries increases? The results are designed to map the sensitivity of emission trajectories to tax-rate escalation schedules. Future research utilizing high-frequency longitudinal data would strengthen these findings by refining the temporal lag between tax implementation and observed emission declines.

=== INTRODUCTION ===
The implementation of carbon taxation represents a primary instrument for internalizing the social cost of carbon and incentivizing the transition to low-carbon energy systems [CIT:author-year-theory]. The central research question concerns the consistency of emission reductions across diverse economic contexts, as the efficacy of such taxes is theorized to depend on the elasticity of substitution between carbon-intensive and green energy sources [CIT:author-year-elasticity]. Because carbon taxes operate through price signals that propagate through complex supply chains, the resulting emission reductions are often heterogeneous across sectors [CIT:author-year-sectoral]. Understanding this heterogeneity is critical for policymakers designing tax schedules that balance environmental targets with economic competitiveness [CIT:author-year-policy]. Prior empirical work suggests that the magnitude of the effect is contingent upon the tax rate and the availability of low-carbon alternatives [CIT:author-year-alternatives].

The landscape of prior syntheses has established a foundation for understanding carbon pricing, yet specific gaps persist. Meta-analyses by Author (Year) [CIT:author-year-meta1] provided an aggregate estimate of emission reductions across OECD nations, finding a general downward trend in emissions following tax adoption [EFFECT=...], [CI=...]. However, that synthesis did not provide a fully powered, pre-specified joint moderator framework for the interaction between tax rates and sectoral leakage [CIT:author-year-meta1]. Similarly, the work of Author (Year) [CIT:author-year-meta2] examined the role of revenue recycling, suggesting that revenue-neutral frameworks may increase public acceptance and sustain higher tax levels [EFFECT=...], [CI=...]. While this work addressed the political economy of carbon taxes, it did not quantify the specific marginal impact of recycling mechanisms on the actual rate of emission decline across different GDP tiers [CIT:author-year-meta2]. Further syntheses have focused on regional clusters, such as the European Union, where the interaction between national taxes and the Emissions Trading System (ETS) creates a complex overlapping regulatory environment [CIT:author-year-eu-cluster]. These regional studies highlight the difficulty of isolating the effect of a standalone tax from broader climate policies [CIT:author-year-eu-cluster].

The present work extends these prior syntheses by applying a rigorous, automated evidence-contract pipeline to stress-test the stability of reported effect sizes across a broader set of jurisdictional variables. Unlike prior reviews that may rely on manual extraction subject to inter-reviewer variance, this pipeline utilizes a deterministic rule-triage and a universal evidence-contract gate to ensure that only records with explicit endpoint and intervention coverage are included [N_ACCEPTED]. This approach is designed to refine the understanding of the "tax-rate threshold," the point at which carbon prices trigger significant technological shifts rather than mere behavioral adjustments [CIT:author-year-threshold]. By partitioning variance by jurisdictional GDP and the presence of border adjustment mechanisms, this analysis is designed to quantify the extent to which "carbon leakage"—the relocation of emissions to untaxed jurisdictions—offsets the domestic gains of a carbon tax [CIT:author-year-leakage]. This refinement allows for a more granular mapping of the efficacy of carbon pricing in emerging versus developed economies, a distinction that remains under-specified in the broader literature [CIT:author-year-emerging].

The scope of this analysis is explicitly limited to the quantitative estimation of emission reductions directly attributable to carbon taxes. A primary non-goal of this study is the evaluation of the qualitative "fairness" or social equity of tax distributions; while these factors are discussed in the literature [CIT:author-year-equity], they do not enter the quantitative pool of the present pipeline. Additionally, this work does not aim to provide a risk-of-bias adjudication using a specific framework for every included study; such per-domain expert adjudication is a designed-for capability of the pipeline, deferred until the corpus accrues k >= 10 contract-passing effects per metric family. The analysis also defers the execution of leave-one-out diagnostics, publication-bias regressions, and trim-and-fill adjustments, as these are designed-for capabilities deferred until a larger k is reached. The pipeline does not perform dual independent extraction or three-reviewer adjudication, relying instead on a single-pass LLM extraction verified by a Researka Tier-2 canonical-fact cross-check overlay.

Furthermore, this synthesis does not intend to model the synergistic effects of carbon taxes when combined with direct subsidies for renewables, unless such combinations are reported as a single intervention in the source text [CIT:author-year-synergy]. The focus remains on the isolated or primary effect of the tax instrument. By restricting the analysis to a single metric family—typically percent change in CO2 equivalent emissions—the pipeline avoids the noise associated with mixing disparate environmental indicators [CIT:author-year-metrics]. The intended contribution is thus a high-fidelity, reproducible map of the carbon tax's impact, providing a benchmark that subsequent longitudinal studies can use to calibrate their models of climate policy efficacy [CIT:author-year-benchmark]. Through this structured approach, the present work seeks to resolve the tension between optimistic policy projections and the varied empirical outcomes reported in fragmented regional studies [CIT:author-year-tension].
