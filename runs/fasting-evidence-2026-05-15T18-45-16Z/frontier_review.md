# Frontier review — fasting

**Snapshot:** 2026-05-15T18-45-16Z
**Strategist model:** mimo-v2.5-pro

## The lens

The collected evidence reveals a pronounced paradox: fasting ameliorates glycemic and metabolic parameters in healthy populations but may worsen them in diabetics, pointing to a critical need for pathology-stratified guidelines rather than universal recommendations. Furthermore, animal studies on gut permeability (e.g., in Ross chickens) hint at unexplored mechanistic pathways that could inform human conditions like NAFLD, but translational gaps persist.

## Already known — do not publish

- Fasting decreases fasting blood sugar in healthy men
- Ramadan fasting reduces BMI and insulin in healthy subjects
- Fasting lowers blood pressure in long-term programs

## Tensions / contradictions

- Fact 2 (FBS decreased in healthy men, P<0.0001) vs. Fact 3 (glucose increased in women with non-insulin dependent diabetes mellitus during Ramadan, P<0.05)
- Fact 10 (IGF-I divergence between lean and obese men after 72h fast) vs. Fact 6 (BP reduction in multidisciplinary fasting without body composition details)
- Fact 4 and 5 (increased intestinal permeability in fasting chickens) contrast with human studies showing metabolic benefits, suggesting species-specific responses

## Evidence gaps

- Standardized protocols for fasting duration (e.g., 24h vs. 72h) in diabetic subgroups, as data from fact 3 and 10 are not directly comparable
- Mechanistic studies linking gut permeability changes (from avian models) to human liver or metabolic outcomes, especially in NAFLD populations where vitamin D supplementation was studied (fact 19)
- Combined interventions like fasting with exercise (fact 13) or specific supplements (garlic, fact 20; magnesium, fact 21) in cancer survivors (fact 16) remain underexplored for synergistic effects

## Reviewer objections to anticipate

- Heterogeneity in fasting protocols (e.g., Ramadan time-restricted vs. 72h continuous fasts) limits comparability and may bias pooled estimates
- Over-reliance on acute outcome measures (e.g., 24h glucose changes) without long-term follow-up data on clinical endpoints like colorectal cancer risk (fact 17)
- Animal studies, such as those in Ross chickens or GLP-1 receptor KO mice (fact 11), may not translate to human physiology, reducing the evidence base for mechanistic claims

## Suggested next extractions

- Extract data on fasting duration subgroups (e.g., <24h, 24-72h, >72h) from studies like fact 10 and fact 6 to identify dose-response relationships
- Focus on specific genetic or biomarker pathways (IGF-1, GLP-1, GIP) from fact 10, 11, 12 in human cohorts with obesity or diabetes
- Harvest trials combining fasting with adjunct therapies (e.g., vitamin D in NAFLD, fact 19; garlic in T2DM, fact 20) to assess interaction effects
